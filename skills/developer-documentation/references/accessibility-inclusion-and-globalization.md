# Accessibility, inclusion, and globalization

Open this reference when checking whether documentation works across abilities, assistive technologies, cultures, and levels of English fluency.

## Accessible content

- Make every instruction and piece of information usable without relying on color, position, shape, sound, hover, or a pointer device.
- Use a logical heading hierarchy, semantic lists and tables, native controls, descriptive labels, and meaningful link text.
- Ensure interactive documentation is keyboard reachable and test important flows with a screen reader when the publishing surface permits it.
- Keep sentences and paragraphs short enough to scan. Put distinguishing information first.
- Avoid forced line breaks, centered or fully justified body text, all-caps prose, and complex punctuation as the only signal of structure.
- Provide transcripts or equivalent text for audio and video. Avoid rapid flashing and supply controls for motion or autoplay.
- Give forms explicit labels, instructions, validation messages, and error recovery that don't depend only on color.

## Inclusive language

- Refer to people in ways that preserve personhood and relevance. Mention identity, disability, age, gender, race, or geography only when it matters to the technical content.
- Use singular _they_ and gender-neutral role names. Avoid binary assumptions.
- Research a community's preferred terminology when identity is relevant; person-first and identity-first preferences vary.
- Use neutral descriptions of disability. Avoid language such as _suffering from_, _victim of_, _wheelchair-bound_, euphemisms, or patronizing alternatives.
- Replace figurative, violent, ableist, or culturally loaded expressions with literal descriptions of behavior.
- Avoid assigning demographic stereotypes to roles in examples. Represent a range of names and contexts without making identity decorative.

## Global readability

- Use direct words in their primary meaning, standard sentence structures, explicit articles, and clear modifiers.
- Avoid idioms, jokes, puns, slang, region-specific sports or seasonal references, and phrasal verbs when a single direct verb is available.
- Keep terminology, capitalization, formatting, and repeated sentence patterns consistent; this improves comprehension and translation.
- Define abbreviations and avoid noun stacks. Expand helper words such as _that_ when they remove ambiguity.
- Don't use a slash to mean _and_ or _or_. Write the intended relationship.
- Use examples that don't require a particular culture, legal system, address format, currency, or measurement system unless the topic requires it.

## Safe example data

- Never use real personal information, credentials, customer names, project IDs, phone numbers, or production endpoints.
- Use `example.com`, `example.org`, or `example.net` for generic domains.
- Use documentation-only IPv4 ranges `192.0.2.0/24`, `198.51.100.0/24`, or `203.0.113.0/24`, and IPv6 range `2001:db8::/32`.
- Use descriptive fictitious project and resource names rather than `foo`, `bar`, and `baz`.
- Use placeholders when a reader must substitute their own value, and explain the required value precisely.
